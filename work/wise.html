Reusable onboarding for regulated journeys

I wrote this for the Wise Group Design Lead role in Global KYC Onboarding. I have not run KYC or identity verification at global scale, and I will not pretend I have. This page shows what I have done that points the same way: design systems, regulated financial products, and leading designers beside engineers. It ends with how I would start.

Reusable components that regional teams assemble into local onboarding journeys, with regulation fixed and friction low, led by someone who stays hands-on.

The role

What I understand you need.

  • Components regional teams can reuse. The brief is a platform of “Lego pieces” that Europe, APAC and the Americas assemble into local onboarding journeys, so a journey takes weeks instead of months.
  • Compliance and low friction together. Regulatory requirements are fixed. The design work is making them feel smooth for the many legitimate customers.
  • A lead who stays hands-on. Partner with the product manager, engineering and analytics leads, work with regional design leaders, and grow a team of designers.
The evidence

Five things I have done that map to this.

01

TSB: a design system and a design function, from zero

I joined days into the 2018 platform migration, led the design of the response, then stayed five years. I built the in-house design function to up to 17 people and built the design system: shared patterns and components so teams stopped solving the same problem differently, and a change could reach the whole app. I worked beside IBM engineers, and with no product managers my team led the roadmap. The app went from 1.8 to 4.6 App Store stars. Read the full case.

02

TSB: friction in sign-up-type moments

Our research and data pointed at onboarding, identity verification and complex servicing as the places people lost confidence. We broke them into smaller steps, made progress visible and said what was happening at each stage. This was one consumer app, not a global KYC platform, but it is the same kind of moment.

03

Vanquis: design inside regulation

As Head of Customer Experience I led a team of 15 across products serving 1.7M+ customers. I built an inclusive-design framework that put accessibility and vulnerability awareness into delivery under FCA rules, and co-authored the Vulnerable Customer Policy. The lesson is that regulation works best as a design input from the start, not a gate at the end.

04

A public design system with accessibility checked in CI

I extracted the palettes from my production platform into a small public design system, ds.yennyotero.com, with three brands from one codebase. Components ask for colours by role, not by value, and each brand supplies its own palette. A Vitest test checks five text roles against three backgrounds at WCAG AA, 4.5:1, on every pull request. Where a brand falls short, the exception is pinned to its current ratio, and a second test flags it once it is fixed. It is strict TypeScript, and the code is public at github.com/flamingointheweb/design-system-showcase.

It is small and it is mine. It shows how I think about reuse and rules that cannot be skipped. It is not evidence of running a platform for dozens of teams.

05

Smaller onboarding, and a complex service

For Bachata Week Cádiz I replaced a 20-minute manual registration with NFC wristband check-in: 344 arrivals over two afternoons. The festival gets about 1,500 applications a year for about 300 places, and had 349 participants from 45 countries in 2026. At Oslo University Hospital I led the service design of the breast cancer diagnostic pathway, cutting time to diagnosis from up to 12 weeks to six days; Norway adopted it as the national standard. That one was a many-stakeholder system, where the evidence and the clinicians had to change it together. Read the full case.

A plan, not a result

How I would start, in the first 90 days.

This is my plan from the job description, before I know your systems. It is not something I have done at Wise.

  • Weeks 1 to 4: listen and map. Audit the current KYC journeys and how each region has varied them. Sit with the product, engineering and analytics leads, and with the regional design leads who will use the platform. Find where customers drop out and where regional teams spend the months.
  • Weeks 3 to 8: separate fixed from flexible. With compliance, map which requirements cannot change and which can. That decides what belongs inside a component and what a region may adapt.
  • Weeks 6 to 12: a small, rules-first set of components. A few reusable pieces with usage rules, content guidance and accessibility checks that run automatically, as in my own design system. Pilot with one region and build one localised journey from them.
  • Measure two things. How long a regional team takes to build a localised journey, and where customers drop out. Those are the numbers the brief points to.
  • Grow the team. Hire and coach designers for the personal, business and future platform teams, while staying close to the work.
What I haven’t done

Where I am short, and what I would learn first

  • I have not shipped a KYC or identity-verification product at global scale, and I have not worked on a platform of Wise’s size.
  • I have not designed for dozens of internal teams at once. My design system serves one platform.
  • I do not know the specific regulatory differences between regions. I would learn them from your compliance and regional teams in the first weeks, before proposing any component.
  • First I would learn how your regional teams actually build journeys today, because the reuse problem is theirs, not mine.

I would be glad to talk it through, and to share my screen.
